\title[Disclosed Channel Structure in BB84]{Beyond Aggregate QBER: The Value of Disclosed Channel Structure in Gate-Based BB84}

% Author metadata is omitted in this repository edition.
\begin{abstract}
Aggregate quantum bit error rate (QBER) mixes errors caused by an eavesdropper
with errors caused by legitimate devices. We ask when disclosed BB84 data
separate those contributions, how accurately they do so, and when retaining
channel structure helps recover bits. Under a gate-level depolarizing model,
a rank criterion characterizes identification from known gate paths.
Standard encoding and a variant without a gate-free path identify the
error of ideal intercept--resend on noiseless devices despite unknown, unequal device
noise. Balanced paths lose that information; four noise-scaling settings
provide a sufficient design. Unknown unscaled readout leaves a combined
disturbance identifiable. Budget-matched simulations over 81 conditions
separate identification from precision. Full operating observations have
lower error in all 27 standard-encoding comparisons, while scaling has
lower error in 25 of 27 comparisons for the variant. A supporting
reconciliation study tests the cost of estimating conditional error rates:
pooling moderates estimation harms, while larger long-block yield gains
arise mainly through rate selection. The results connect what observations
identify to their precision and finite-sample utility. Neither diagnostic
disturbance nor reconciliation yield is a secure-key rate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
An increase in BB84's quantum bit error rate (QBER) can reflect device noise,
channel disturbance, or an attack. Distinguishing those explanations matters for
device monitoring, checking a calibration, and interpreting a trusted-device model.
The aggregate error rate alone can be ambiguous: Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity} shows
two modeled channels with the same QBER of about 3.56\%, one with intercept--resend
and one without. Yet their errors are distributed differently across the public
basis labels. Information that disappears in the average is still present in
the disclosed observations.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.97\linewidth]{images/aggregate_ambiguity.pdf}
\caption{The motivating ambiguity: equal aggregate QBER, different causes.
Both channels have operating QBER 3.56\% in the ideal-Eve depolarizing model.
One combines lower device noise with interception; the other has device noise alone.
Left: their two basis error rates distinguish them without another acquisition.
Right: changing legitimate-device noise also separates their responses.
Dashed segments and open points indicate extrapolation to noiseless gates.
Exact response laws are checked against density-state propagation.}
\Description{The two states have different basis-specific rates despite equal
aggregate QBER. Their noise-scaling curves intersect at scale one but have residuals
of 2.5 percent and zero.}
\label{fig:ambiguity}
\end{figure}

This paper asks what is gained by preserving that channel structure. We follow
one sequence of questions. First, do the disclosed observations identify the
disturbance of interest? Second, how accurately can it be estimated from a fixed
number of transmitted signals? Finally, when the same kind of structure is
estimated from a limited public sample, does it improve recovery of the retained
bits? The last question closes the argument: information can be present in a
channel yet cost more to estimate and disclose than it returns in recovered bits.

Our diagnostic target is the \emph{residual disturbance} $e_0$: the QBER that
remains when the specified legitimate-device gate noise is removed. With ideal
intercept--resend and noiseless readout, it is the error that the attack would
produce on noiseless devices. We ask about this quantity within an explicit
physical model. Reconciliation uses the error probabilities at the actual
operating setting, where device noise is still present. Both tasks draw on the
same conditional channel, but require information about different features of it.

Gate-based BB84 makes the role of observation structure explicit. Different
preparation and measurement paths encounter different numbers of noisy gates.
We derive a rank criterion for identifying the device and attack parameters
from those paths, together with the weaker condition needed to identify only
$e_0$. Standard BB84 and a variant with no gate-free path satisfy the criterion.
Balancing all paths removes the identifying contrast; four noise-scaling settings
then provide a sufficient design. Unknown unscaled readout changes the
identifiable quantity to a combined disturbance.

The experiments put a resource cost on this structural result. We compare
already disclosed observations with additional noise-scaling acquisitions and
a paid calibration reference under the same signal budget. An 81-condition
study shows that identification alone does not determine the best acquisition:
full operating observations are more accurate throughout the standard circuit's
tested grid, while a second circuit generally favors scaling and reverses that
ordering in two weak-interception conditions. The supporting reconciliation
experiment tests the remaining step, from observable structure to finite-sample
utility. Pooling unreliable contrasts improves its measured benefit--harm
balance; at long blocks, the larger yield differences arise through rate choices.

The contributions are therefore linked by the cost of using disclosed information:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An identification criterion.} A log-linear path representation
separates identification of all model parameters from identification of the
residual, and classifies the BB84 implementations considered here.
\item \textbf{A budget-matched precision comparison.} Complete operating data,
matched scaling ranges, calibration, and a budget/interception grid test how
structural information translates into estimation accuracy.
\item \textbf{A finite-sample utility test.} Circuit controls, frozen pooling,
and transfer separate the benefit of an estimated prior from the effects of
sample disclosure and rate selection.
\end{enumerate}
The established tools are conditional entropy, likelihood, extrapolation,
pooling and soft decoding. The contribution is their application to a common,
explicitly observed BB84 channel: a general identification test, its circuit
consequences, and controlled evidence for the cost of exploiting that structure.

Section~\ref{sec:setting} defines the data and physical model.
Sections~\ref{sec:diagnostics} and~\ref{sec:precision} establish identification
and compare precision. Section~\ref{sec:results} tests the cost of using
conditional information for recovery. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws the
resulting decision principles and scope together.

\section{Disclosed Observations and the Physical Model}
\label{sec:setting}
\label{sec:mechanism}


\subsection{What is observed, and what is being estimated?}
We consider BB84~\cite{bennett1984} with uniformly random bits and bases,
independent single-qubit signals, and an authenticated noiseless classical
channel. Alice's bit is $A$, Bob's measured bit is $B$, and their matching
sifted basis is $Z\in\{\mathrm r,\mathrm d\}$. We write $f$ for a controlled
noise-scaling factor. The \emph{operating setting} is $f=1$, before any extra
scaling intervention.

A diagnostic acquisition transmits $T$ signals and discloses all sifted
observations. Retaining their bit and basis labels gives the four conditional
error rates $Q_{az}=\Pr(B\ne A\mid A=a,Z=z)$, together with their observed
counts. Averaging over Alice's bit leaves two basis rates; averaging again
leaves aggregate QBER. Table~\ref{tab:observations} defines the observation
sets used throughout the paper. These are different summaries of an acquisition,
so their comparison measures the cost of discarding labels.

The conditional channel is $W_f(b\mid a,z)$. Under uniform inputs,
$P(A=a,B=b,Z=z)=W_1(b\mid a,z)/4$ at the operating setting.
Diagnostics infer a property of this channel's noise response. Recovery uses
$W_1$ itself: a frame discloses $S$ sifted bits to estimate its error probabilities
and retains $n$ bits for decoding. Bob can use the public bases and his local
outcomes on those retained positions. This difference in what is disclosed
determines the estimators available in each task.

The distinction can be seen without choosing an estimator. If two acquisitions
contain the same total number of errors but place them in different basis or
input-bit groups, their aggregate records are identical. Their full records
are different, and a model can use that difference to distinguish possible
causes. Conversely, if two physical explanations give the same probability
in every retained group, collecting more signals at that setting only measures
the shared probabilities more precisely. Identification concerns this second
ambiguity; sampling precision concerns uncertainty about probabilities that
the observation design can distinguish.

\subsection{Complete gate paths create the observable contrast}
Figure~\ref{fig:circuits} includes both preparation and receiver rotations.
The \emph{standard} circuit prepares rectilinear zero without a gate, uses
$X$ for rectilinear one, and $H$ or $XH$ for the diagonal states.
Scheme A adds an identity to every preparation, removing the gate-free path
while preserving unequal exposures. Scheme B uses two preparation gates for
every state. Bob still applies $H$ before diagonal measurement; B+ adds an
identity before rectilinear measurement and balances the complete paths.
Each identity is an explicit noise site in the circuit exports.

For homogeneous depolarization
$\mathcal D_p(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+pI/2$ after each gate, covariance under ideal
gates gives the error probability for a path with $k$ noisy gates:
\begin{equation}
 q_k(p)=\frac{1-(1-p)^k}{2}.
 \label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
The complete counts for the two bits in each basis are $(0,1;2,3)$ in Standard,
$(1,2;3,4)$ in A, $(2,2;3,3)$ in B, and $(3,3;3,3)$ in B+.
Thus equal preparation counts alone do not equalize the measurement paths.
The basis error probability is the average over its two bit paths.
Independent density-state calculations check these probabilities and the
two-hop intercept--resend response.

The variants expose different sources of information. A retains path contrast
without a noiseless preparation. B retains a basis contrast generated by Bob's
rotation. B+ removes that contrast. All four enter the circuit recovery controls;
the precision grid compares Standard, A and B+ to cover a direct residual path,
identification through several noisy paths, and a fully balanced nonidentifying
operating acquisition. Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics} also classifies B analytically.
The corresponding record identifier for Standard is \texttt{PRIMARY}.

\subsection{Comparisons and resource accounting}
The analysis follows the order in Figure~\ref{fig:overview}. First we study exact
conditional probabilities, before sampling error. Next, diagnostic comparisons
charge every transmitted signal, including those lost in sifting. A calibration
reference and every scaling setting count toward the same $T$. The scaling law
and candidate acquisition designs are specified before their outcomes are generated.
Finally, recovery comparisons charge the disclosed sample, syndrome and verification
bits, and distinguish a common fixed code from separately selected rates.
Appendix~\ref{app:inventory} gives the complete experimental inventory.

Each diagnostic condition has 500 replicate blocks. A block contains the
independently budgeted acquisitions specified by its protocol; estimators of
one acquisition may reuse its counts. Reconciliation methods are paired on
the same frames and code when the comparison fixes disclosure. Independent
development, confirmation and transfer records protect the reported comparisons
from tuning on their evaluation outcomes. The confirmation grid repeats the
development conditions; unseen code libraries supply the separate transfer test.

The supplement supplies the full counts, matrices, parameter bounds and seeds.
Independent density-state responses, reference decoders, numerical optimizers
and retained-record checks verify the implementation. Table captions identify
the uncertainty unit for each comparison: acquisition replicates, paired frames,
or independent matrix libraries. Detailed estimators and checks are collected
with their numerical results in the appendices, leaving the main text to follow
the information question.

\subsection{Relation to diagnostic and reconciliation methods}
Zero-noise extrapolation acquires observations at additional noise settings
to estimate an idealized quantity~\cite{temme2017,li2017,giurgicatiron2020};
its precision depends on the acquisition design~\cite{krebsbach2022}.
Fiorini et al.~\cite{fiorini2024} estimate interception density in noisy BB84,
and their subsequent work uses it for threshold-based detection~\cite{fiorini2026}.
Our comparison first asks which disclosed observations identify the target,
then compares full transitions, basis averages, scaling and a reference
acquisition under explicit device assumptions and equal budgets.
The published interception-density formula is evaluated on its native law and
on the gate-response law in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostic}.

Qi et al.~\cite{qi2007dualdet} alternate a quiet detector that bounds Eve's
information with a fast detector used for key generation. Here the distinction
is between summaries of disclosed gate-path observations and additional
noise interventions. The target is a modeled residual under unknown device
noise; the rank test characterizes what those observations determine.

Low-density parity-check (LDPC) syndrome reconciliation and rate adaptation are established in
QKD~\cite{elkouss2009,elkouss2011,blind2012,martinez2013}. Public-channel
metrics, puncturing and shortening already provide structured decoding
information~\cite{mesiti2010,blind2012}. Simultaneous basis and information
reconciliation~\cite{martinezmateo2024} acts at another protocol stage, while
soft metrics for discrete-modulation continuous-variable QKD concern a different
measurement channel~\cite{origlia2026}. Our supporting experiment studies the
cost of estimating reliability from an already-public basis label.
Following practical reconciliation studies~\cite{borisov2023,treeviriyanupab2024},
it accounts for both disclosure and recovery, with controls separating the
prior, code and rate policy.

\section{Which Observations Identify the Disturbance?}
\label{sec:diagnostics}
\subsection{The residual target and its physical assumptions}
Eve independently intercepts fraction $r$ of the signals, measures in a
uniformly random basis and resends the resulting state. Let $p_A,p_B$ denote
the depolarizing probabilities after Alice's and Bob's gates, and $p_E$ the
noise on Eve's operations. Scaling multiplies the specified legitimate-device
gate noises by $f$, keeping the attack and any fixed Eve noise unchanged.
For the standard paths the aggregate response is
\begin{equation}
 Q_f=(1-r)q_0(fp_A,fp_B)+r q_I(fp_A,fp_B,p_E),\qquad
 e_0=\lim_{f\to 0}Q_f=r\,q_I(0,0,p_E).
 \label{eq:separatedresponse}
\end{equation}
Here $q_0$ and $q_I$ are the exact unintercepted and intercepted path averages
derived in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostic}. The limit defines the QBER remaining
after controllable legitimate gate noise is removed. With ideal Eve and
noiseless readout, $e_0=r/4$. The identification result below uses that case;
Table~\ref{tab:assumptions} locates the additional controls within the same notation.

\subsection{Why the aggregate can fail when labeled data succeed}
Under ideal Eve and equal device noise, the standard-circuit response reduces to
\begin{equation}
 Q_f=\frac r4+\left(1-\frac r2\right)q_0(fp).
 \label{eq:attackequal}
\end{equation}
One aggregate value $Q_1$ admits a continuum of device-noise and interception
pairs: $r=[Q_1-q_0(p)]/[1/4-q_0(p)/2]$ wherever the parameters are physical.
This explains the ambiguity in Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity}. More observations
at that same aggregate setting reduce sampling error but leave the structural
ambiguity intact.

Retaining the basis changes the question. The two operating rates satisfy
\begin{equation}
 Q_z=\frac r4+\left(1-\frac r2\right)q_z(p),\qquad
 q_{\mathrm r}(p)=\frac p4,\quad
 q_{\mathrm d}(p)=\frac{5p-4p^2+p^3}{4}.
 \label{eq:basis}
\end{equation}
Writing $\eta=(Q_{\mathrm d}-Q_{\mathrm r})/(1/2-Q_{\mathrm r})$ gives
\begin{equation}
 p=1-\sqrt{1-\eta},\qquad
 r=\frac{4Q_{\mathrm r}-p}{1-p/2}.
 \label{eq:basisinverse}
\end{equation}
The inverse is unique on $0\leq p<1$, $0\leq r\leq1$. Thus one basis-resolved
acquisition already identifies the residual under the equal-device model.
Finite counts can make this inverse unstable or outside the physical domain;
Appendix~\ref{app:range} specifies the corresponding estimator and guards.
The next result shows what happens when the two device noises are independently unknown.

\subsection{A general criterion from gate-path counts}
For ideal Eve, a path containing $k_A$ Alice gates and $k_B$ Bob gates has
\begin{equation}
 Q_{az}(f)=\frac12-\left(\frac12-\frac r4\right)
 (1-fp_A)^{k_A(a,z)}(1-fp_B)^{k_B(a,z)}.
 \label{eq:attack}
\end{equation}
Let $F=\{f_1,\ldots,f_J\}$ be the positive scaling settings,
$f_{\max}=\max(\{1\}\cup F)$, and
$\Theta_F=\{(p_A,p_B,r):0\leq p_A,p_B<1/f_{\max},\ 0\leq r\leq1\}$.
These bounds keep every path contraction positive. A target is
\emph{identified} when any two parameter points producing the same observations
have the same target value.

\Needspace{15\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}[Identification by gate-path structure]
\label{prop:identification}
Suppose the operating observations retain the error probability for each of
the known paths $j=1,\ldots,K$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:attack}.
Form the matrix $M$ with rows $(1,k_{A,j},k_{B,j})$.
On $\Theta_F$, these observations identify all three parameters
$(p_A,p_B,r)$ if and only if $\operatorname{rank}M=3$.
They identify the residual $e_0=r/4$ if and only if
$(1,0,0)$ belongs to the row space of $M$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put $u=1-p_A$, $v=1-p_B$, $d=1-r/2$ and $s_j=1-2Q_j(1)$.
Then $s_j=d\,u^{k_{A,j}}v^{k_{B,j}}>0$, so
\[
 \log\mathbf s=M\eta,\qquad
 \eta=(\log d,\log u,\log v)^{\mathsf T}.
\]
The componentwise logarithm and the parameter transformation are one-to-one
on the domain. Rank three therefore gives a unique parameter vector.
If the rank is smaller, a nonzero vector in $\ker M$ gives a direction along
which the observations remain constant; sufficiently small perturbations of
an interior point stay in the domain. This proves the first statement.

The residual depends only on $\eta_1=\log d$. It is fixed by $M\eta$
exactly when every vector in $\ker M$ has zero first coordinate, equivalently
when $(1,0,0)$ is in the row space. In that case, weights satisfying
$w^{\mathsf T}M=(1,0,0)$ give $d=\prod_j s_j^{w_j}$ and
$e_0=(1-d)/2$. Otherwise a small interior perturbation along an appropriate
null vector changes the residual while preserving all observations.
\end{proof}

The criterion concerns the target as well as the number of unknowns.
For example, paths with counts $(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$ identify the residual
through the first path, although Bob's unexposed noise remains undetermined.
Distinct monomials alone do not guarantee rank three. This distinction lets
the same criterion classify arbitrary path collections before a fitting
procedure is selected.

Geometrically, rank three means that the observed count pairs
$(k_A,k_B)$ do not all lie on one affine line. The logarithm turns a
multiplicative physical response into a linear system: its intercept is the
logarithm of the attack contraction, and its two slopes encode the device contractions.
Paths with different total exposure can still be redundant if they change
Alice's and Bob's counts together in the same proportion. The row-space
condition asks the more focused question of whether some weighted combination
cancels both device contributions while retaining the intercept.
This is why identifying a target can require less information than estimating
every nuisance parameter.

The result also distinguishes structural redundancy from noisy estimation.
Repeating a path adds observations but repeats a row of $M$, so it can improve
precision without changing the rank. Adding a path with a new exposure pattern
can change the rank and remove an ambiguity. For a proposed implementation,
the known path counts therefore give a direct check before optimization or
Monte Carlo sampling. The finite-data estimator must still account for
binomial uncertainty and physical bounds; the logarithmic argument establishes
identification from exact probabilities.

\begin{corollary}[Consequences for the BB84 implementations]
\label{cor:circuits}
Full operating observations identify all three parameters in Standard and A.
They do not identify $e_0$ in B or B+. For B+, four distinct scaling settings
within $\Theta_F$ suffice to identify $e_0$ from aggregate rates.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
In the order $(0\mathrm r,1\mathrm r,0\mathrm d,1\mathrm d)$, the contraction
vectors are $d(1,u,uv,u^2v)$ for Standard and $d(u,u^2,u^2v,u^3v)$ for A.
Their augmented path matrices have rank three. Explicitly, Standard gives
$d=s_{0r}$, $u=s_{1r}/d$, $v=s_{0d}/(du)$; A gives
$u=s_{1r}/s_{0r}$, $d=s_{0r}/u$, $v=s_{0d}/(du^2)$.

B gives $d(u^2,u^2,u^2v,u^2v)$: the ratio determines $v$, while
$du^2$ leaves interception confounded with Alice's noise.
B+ gives only $s=du^2v$. Both fail the residual row-space condition.
For B+ across scales,
$Q_f=\{1-d(1-fp_A)^2(1-fp_B)\}/2$ has degree at most three.
Four distinct exact values determine its intercept,
$Q_0=(1-d)/2=e_0$.
\end{proof}

Four-setting interpolation is a sufficient design. Three values do not
determine a general cubic: adding $\kappa\prod_{j=1}^3(f-f_j)$ preserves
them while changing its intercept at positive $f_j$. A smaller design would
require a separate argument using the physical coefficient restrictions,
and its numerical stability would remain a finite-data question.

The standard circuit has the particularly direct observation
\begin{equation}
 \Pr(B\ne A\mid A=0,Z=\mathrm r)=e_0.
 \label{eq:subset}
\end{equation}
It uses an expected $T/8$ of the transmitted signals. The full acquisition
also contains the other paths, which may improve precision. Scheme A
identifies the same target entirely through noisy paths. B demonstrates
that a visible basis contrast can still be insufficient to separate the
attack from device noise. B+ removes even that contrast, making the need
for an additional observation design transparent.

These circuits illustrate two ways to expose the same target. Standard's
zero-gate path observes the attack disturbance directly, but only a fraction
of transmitted signals take that path and survive sifting. A observes no
such path: its residual is inferred by comparing contractions with different
exposures. The two designs can therefore have the same identification status
and different sampling sensitivity. Balanced B+ takes the opposite case to
its limit, since every operating path observes the same product of device
and attack contractions. Scaling changes the device factors while holding the
attack contraction fixed, creating the contrast absent from its operating data.

\subsection{Unscaled readout changes what is identified}
An unknown common symmetric readout flip $0\leq c<1/2$ multiplies every
contraction by $(1-2c)$. The same algebra then identifies the combined target
\begin{equation}
 e_c=c+(1-2c)r/4
 \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where it previously identified $e_0$. Different pairs $(c,r)$ can give the
same contraction, so attribution requires further information about readout.
For known $c$, the inverse is $r=4(e_c-c)/(1-2c)$.
This distinction will explain the readout controls in
Section~\ref{sec:precision}: precise estimation of a combined disturbance
and correct attribution of its source are separate achievements.

The confounding persists at every scale under this common readout model.
The observed path contraction is
$(1-2c)(1-r/2)(1-fp_A)^{k_A}(1-fp_B)^{k_B}$.
Scaling can separate the varying device factors from their fixed multiplier,
but the multiplier contains both readout and interception. A more precise
estimate of that multiplier therefore improves estimation of $e_c$ while
leaving its decomposition unresolved. An external characterization of $c$
changes the information available to the inference problem and must be
included explicitly when comparing acquisition strategies.

\subsection{What a noise-scaling intervention requires}
The transformation $p_A\mapsto fp_A$, $p_B\mapsto fp_B$ is a characterized
intervention in the simulations. A model-level realization adds randomized
Pauli noise after each legitimate-device gate. Since
$\mathcal D_\eta\circ\mathcal D_p=\mathcal D_{p+\eta-p\eta}$, choosing
\begin{equation}
 \eta(f,p)=\frac{(f-1)p}{1-p},\qquad f\geq1,\quad fp<1
 \label{eq:injection}
\end{equation}
gives the prescribed channel, with injected probabilities $\eta/4$ for each
of $X,Y,Z$ and $1-3\eta/4$ for identity. A physical implementation therefore
needs a characterized baseline, calibrated injection and an error model for
the injection operations.

Gate folding and pulse stretching provide different controls
\cite{giurgicatiron2020,temme2017}. Identical independent noise applications
combine as $1-(1-p)^k$, agreeing with $kp$ only to first order; stationary
exponential decay under stretching has the same nonlinear form.
The intervention must therefore be matched to the response model before
its acquisition budget can be compared. We now make that comparison within
the specified scaling law, first against basis observations and then against
the complete operating data.

\section{How Much Precision Does Additional Acquisition Buy?}
\label{sec:precision}
The accuracy measure is mean absolute error (MAE) of the estimated residual,
reported in percentage points (pp). Empirical interval coverage is the fraction
of replicate intervals containing the generating target, and interval width
measures the remaining uncertainty. These three summaries answer different
questions: an estimator can have a small average error yet understate its
uncertainty, or attain high coverage with intervals too broad to resolve a
small disturbance. We report them together where the target is identified.
For a nonidentified operating model, we retain the compatible residual set.

\subsection{Matched ranges separate the effect of scaling from setting count}
The first comparison uses the equal-device model identified by the two basis
rates. A single operating acquisition, two- or four-setting aggregate scaling,
and calibrated inversion each pay the same transmitted budget $T$.
Calibration assigns 30\% to an independent Eve-free reference and 70\% to the
attacked setting. The 48 nominal conditions cross four noise strengths, four
interception fractions and three budgets; nine interior conditions at $T=8000$
test interpolation without retuning. Each condition has 500 replicate blocks.

For scaling, the maximum tested factor $f_{\max}$ is 2.5, 4 or 8.
At each maximum, a finite minimax Fisher search selects the locations and
positive signal allocations before sampling. The estimators use weighted
least squares (WLS) with Jeffreys plug-in variances and signed interception
estimates. A direct basis inverse supplies a sensitivity comparison on the
same basis counts. Appendix~\ref{app:range} gives the complete grid, finite
search, parameter bounds and numerical results.

Changing the maximum scale changes more than the number of measured points.
At a narrow range, several responses may be close enough that estimating
an intercept requires subtracting noisy quantities with similar means.
Spreading the settings can improve separation of the device response from
the residual, while allocating more signals to a setting reduces its
sampling variance. The Fisher design balances these effects within the
specified response law. Matching the range and optimizing the allocation
within each candidate family makes the comparison about the information
purchased by the acquisition, rather than about an arbitrarily chosen
spacing or number of settings.

both two and four settings. The number of settings alone therefore explains
little of their difference.

The calibration comparator buys a different observation: a reference known
to contain no interception. Under the same equal-device model, its reference
QBER estimates the device contribution, which is then removed from the attacked
QBER through Eq.~\eqref{eq:attackequal}. Its precision includes uncertainty
from both acquisitions. Scaling instead changes legitimate-device noise while
requiring the attack to remain stationary. Both routes supply information
beyond a single aggregate attacked observation, but the assumptions that make
the information available differ. The close errors at the widest range show
why calibrated inversion is an essential comparator for evaluating the value
of acquiring a scaled response.

The small ordering at the widest range changes on the interior grid and when
the direct inverse replaces the constrained basis estimator. Table~\ref{tab:validation}
retains the exact differences and Monte Carlo intervals. The practical lesson
is to compare complete acquisition designs---range, allocation and estimator---under
the same budget. The next comparison asks whether averaging over Alice's bit
has already discarded useful information.

\subsection{Full operating data change the comparison across circuits}
The cross-circuit study uses $T=8000$ and 500 replicates for each of three device
pairs at interception fraction $r=0.2$. Subset, basis and full estimators reuse
one operating acquisition. A separate acquisition uses scaling factors
$(1,3,3.5,4)$ with signal shares $(0.70,0.05,0.05,0.20)$.
This design, whose maximum factor is four, is transferred unchanged across
circuits. Full and scaling fits estimate Alice and Bob noise independently;
the basis fit imposes equality. Physical bounds are
$p_A,p_B\in[0,0.24975]$ and $r\in[0,1]$.

The subset uses Wilson intervals~\cite{wilson1927}. Binomial likelihood
profiles give nominal 95\% residual intervals for the other identified fits.
Empirical coverage checks the effect of finite samples and parameter
boundaries~\cite{fischer2021}. In B+, the operating likelihood instead
provides a set of compatible residuals.

Table~\ref{tab:crosscircuit} shows three different outcomes from the same
resource comparison. In Standard, full data improve on the subset and are
more accurate than scaling in every device pair. Keeping Alice's disclosed bit
also removes the equal-device restriction of the basis fit. In A, the target

these conditions. In B+, operating data leave a range of possible residuals,
while scaling supplies an identified estimate. The path classification
therefore predicts which comparisons are meaningful; finite data determine
their precision.

The equal-noise restriction is particularly consequential here. Basis
averages retain two observed rates. Under one shared device-noise parameter,
those rates can separate noise and interception through Eq.~\eqref{eq:basisinverse}.
Allowing Alice and Bob to differ introduces a third unknown. The full
acquisition retains the input-bit contrast that the basis averages discarded,
and Standard and A satisfy the rank criterion for those separate parameters.
Thus the full comparator answers a stronger modeling question using labels
already present in the same disclosed acquisition. The equality-constrained
basis fit remains informative as a sensitivity comparison when its assumption
holds, and its mismatched-device errors show the cost when it does not.

\subsection{Budget and interception determine how far that ordering transfers}
\label{sec:precisiongrid}
The precision grid crosses these three circuits and device pairs with
$T\in\{2000,8000,32000\}$ and $r\in\{0.02,0.10,0.20\}$.
All 81 conditions have 500 fresh replicate blocks, including conditions
that repeat the preceding cross-circuit comparison. Full and scaling
acquisitions are independent and each receives the complete budget.
The scale locations, allocations, likelihood, bounds and interval procedure
are fixed throughout.

For the 54 identified Standard/A comparisons, define
$\Delta_{\mathrm{MAE}}=\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{full}}-
\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{scaling}}$; positive values favor scaling.
Figure~\ref{fig:precisiongrid} gives the point estimates and pointwise 95\%
Monte Carlo intervals. Filled markers also exclude zero under simultaneous

B+ scaling maintains empirical profile coverage between 94.8\% and 99.6\%.
At the smallest budget and weakest interception, its median intervals are
several times wider than the residual itself. Coverage and resolving a small
disturbance are therefore different performance requirements. The complete
bias, width and boundary summaries allow that distinction to be assessed
alongside the accuracy comparison.

Interception level affects what a given absolute uncertainty means. A broad
interval around a large residual can still distinguish it from a small
background, whereas the same width can obscure a weak disturbance. Increasing
$T$ improves the available counting precision within each fixed model, but
the grid also shows that the preferred acquisition can depend on where that
precision is needed. The weak-interception reversals in A therefore matter
even though most of its cells favor scaling: they locate conditions where the
extra intervention does not repay its acquisition cost. For B+, the relevant
comparison starts earlier, with obtaining an identified residual at all.

\subsection{Checking precision and interpreting its target}
Two supplementary checks help interpret these results. Prediction at an
unfitted scaling setting can look adequate even when an imposed device-equality
assumption distorts the residual (Appendix~\ref{app:range}).
Conversely, profiling the residual separately from the device parameters
can give much narrower target uncertainty despite broad nuisance profiles
(Appendix~\ref{app:profiles}). The reported intervals are assessed by coverage
on their retained cohorts. In these fits the device parameters can move together
along a broad likelihood ridge while the residual changes much less. Profiling
the target follows that ridge and reoptimizes the nuisance parameters at each
proposed residual. The boundary audit distinguishes this behavior from an
interval that merely stops at an allowed target limit. This provides a concrete
reason to examine uncertainty in the quantity of interest separately from
uncertainty in its fitted device parameters.

The readout controls illustrate why the target definition matters.
Full observations track the combined residual $e_c$ when an unscaled
readout error is present, while intervals for the interception-only value
undercover. The reference and stress controls also vary calibration offsets,
scaling curvature, fixed Eve noise and drift (Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostic}).
Together they show the value of preserving observations and of checking the
physical assumptions that connect those observations to a target.

The diagnostic results establish what can be learned, and at what sampling
cost. Recovery now asks the complementary question: when an operating-channel
contrast is available, does estimating it from a sacrificed sample return
enough correctly recovered bits to justify its cost?

\section{When Does Disclosed Structure Improve Recovery?}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{From observable contrast to an estimated decoder prior}
Conditional information is useful to a decoder only if it can be estimated
well enough from the public sample. The population basis benefit is
$G=H(A\mid B)-H(A\mid B,Z)$~\cite{slepian1973,elkouss2011}.
For asymmetric channels, Bob's own bit can supply additional error-location
information, measured by $D_B=I(A\oplus B;B\mid Z)$.
Appendix~\ref{app:information} gives the identities and physical controls.
These benchmarks specify the available information; the following experiment
measures the cost of learning it.

A frame contains $n+S$ sifted bits. Alice discloses $S$ for estimating error
probabilities and sends an $m$-row syndrome for the retained word.
Bob combines that syndrome with his bits and the public bases. Independent
64-bit Toeplitz verification controls acceptance and any retry.
For exact-recovery indicator $I_{\mathrm{corr}}$, the outcome is
\begin{equation}
 Y=\frac{n-m-64}{n+S}I_{\mathrm{corr}},\qquad
 \mathrm{FER}=1-\mathbb E[I_{\mathrm{corr}}].
 \label{eq:yield}
\end{equation}
Mean $Y$ charges sample, syndrome and verification bits, so it evaluates
estimation and recovery together.

We compare a pooled error estimate with basis-conditioned estimates that
shrink toward the same pooled centre. The circuit study uses a positive-part
shrinkage rule. A separate pilot learns a sample-only pooling table that
chooses how much contrast to retain; it is frozen before confirmation.
The decoder receives estimated operating error probabilities from its own
sample. Keeping a common pooled centre ensures that the comparison tests
how much basis contrast to retain rather than giving one prior an extra
smoothing advantage. The selected weight is a function only of the disclosed
sample; the true channel parameters are available solely to oracle comparators.
Appendix~\ref{app:reconmethods} specifies the priors, clipping,
code construction, calibration and statistical comparisons.

For the controlled basis channels, $Q$ is the population average error rate
and $a$ specifies its relative contrast: $p_r=Q(1-a)$ and $p_d=Q(1+a)$.
Thus $a=0$ removes the basis benefit while keeping the disclosed label and its
estimation cost. Belief propagation (BP) decodes the syndrome; the circuit
study also tests an ordered-statistics decoding (OSD) retry.
The evidence has three levels (Table~\ref{tab:recoveryoverview}).
Circuit controls test whether complete-path contrast helps at a common code.
A repeated-grid confirmation tests whether a frozen rule can moderate
estimation harms. Transfer to new code libraries and longer blocks then
separates the prior's effect from the rate policy. This sequence tests
whether information survives each additional practical cost.

\subsection{Pooling preserves useful contrasts while limiting estimation cost}
The circuit result survives both the absence of a gate-free path and a stronger
decoder. In A at 10\% QBER, matched-code BP with an ordered-statistics retry
reduces failures from 28 to 13 out of 2000 frames. The code is the
shrinkage-selected $m=650$ matrix, chosen on independent calibration data.
At pooled's selected $m=700$ code, both priors have zero failures.
The benefit therefore depends on the operating point
(Appendix~\ref{app:circuit}, Table~\ref{tab:matched}).

The separately selected BP operating policies also trade disclosure against
reliability: their +3.94 pp yield difference comes with 19 failures versus
zero. Shrinkage's FER is 0.95\%, but its Wilson upper bound is 1.48\%, above
the 1\% design criterion. Replacing gate noise by isotropic link noise at
fixed QBER removes the basis contrast and can reverse the fixed-code
advantage. These controls motivate returning toward pooling when the sample
contains little reliable contrast.

The frozen pooling rule does so on the 75-condition confirmation grid.
It has 25 Holm-supported gains and no loss passing that corrected threshold,
while separate estimation has more supported harms than gains.
The worst selected-rule point difference is still negative; absence of a
corrected significant harm does not establish dominance.
The 37,500 confirmation frames and matrix seed are new, but the pilot's
QBER, asymmetry, sample-size grid and syndrome lengths are repeated.
Appendix~\ref{app:confirmation} gives the complete table and heatmap.
Generalization is tested separately below.

\subsection{Long-block gains depend mainly on rate selection}
\label{sec:transfer}
Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} compares Gallager and irregular constructions on
five unseen matrix libraries. At the longest block length, fixed-code
selected-prior effects are near zero. Allowing the two learned policies to
choose their own syndrome lengths produces larger gains in strong-asymmetry
conditions. Those gains measure the prior and selector together.
The irregular pipeline's yield accounting assigns essentially all of its
long-block gain to reduced disclosure, with a small loss from failed recovery
(Table~\ref{tab:yieldaccounting}).

Two controls complete the information-cost picture. With a fixed total block,
the largest disclosed sample reduces recovered yield despite improving the
available estimation data (Table~\ref{tab:sample}). At matched QBER,
asymmetric readout benefits from conditioning on Bob's bit rather than the
basis, while homogeneous noise can make the extra estimated parameters
harmful (Table~\ref{tab:physical}). The useful label follows the channel
structure; whether it is worth estimating follows the finite-sample budget.

\section{Implications and Scope}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Preserve structure before paying to acquire or estimate more}
The results support a common order of decisions. First specify the target and
retain the labels already available for estimating it. The rank criterion then
tests whether those observations separate the relevant physical effects.
Only after identification is established does an accuracy comparison decide
how to spend additional signals. Finally, when estimated structure is used
for recovery, evaluate the complete cost in disclosed and lost bits.

This order explains the apparently different circuit outcomes. Standard
already exposes the residual directly and benefits from using its remaining
paths. A removes the direct path but retains enough contrast to identify the
parameters, leaving acquisition choice to a precision comparison.
B retains a basis contrast without separating Alice's noise from interception.
B+ removes the operating contrast altogether, and scaling supplies the
missing observations. Conditional variation is therefore valuable relative
to a particular target, rather than simply because it is visible.

The recovery results provide the finite-sample counterpart. A population
contrast can support better priors, but estimating a weak contrast can
spend the available gain. Pooling addresses that estimation cost.
The distinction between fixed-code and rate-policy outcomes then exposes
the separate contribution of disclosure choices. Across both uses of the
channel, more information must be evaluated through the cost and objective
of the task that consumes it.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{Physical model and intervention.}
The identification result assumes known gate paths, independent stationary
depolarization and the stated intercept--resend model. Unknown readout
identifies a combined disturbance; drift can remove a single stationary
target. The thermal and readout controls test recovery priors, while the
diagnostic response remains the specified depolarizing law.
Time-correlated noise can change its spectral response under amplification
\cite{schultz2022}. Crosstalk during stretched pulses, gate-dependent leakage
and pulse distortion could likewise introduce systematic scale dependence.
More shots reduce sampling error without removing such model bias.
Hardware use requires response checks, a suitable noise model and accounting
for characterization cost, extra duration and calibration uncertainty.
If characterization determines $p_A,p_B$, that information belongs in the
operating-data comparator too.

\paragraph{Finite design and interval calibration.}
The acquisition search covers declared finite ranges and allocations.
Interpolation, one held-out setting and three precision-study layouts test
specific transfer questions. Monte Carlo intervals quantify uncertainty
within those fixed designs. Profile coverage is measured under the fitted
physical bounds; nuisance constraints can influence interval width even when
the residual endpoints do not touch their limits. The WLS/profile comparison
changes loss and bounds as well as interval construction. The smallest
interception cases also show that good coverage can coexist with broad
intervals; practical detection would require an explicit decision criterion.

\paragraph{Recovery implementation and transfer.}
The codes support a mechanism study. Circuit-study $f_{\mathrm{EC}}$ values
around 1.5--3.3 and excess long-block disclosure leave room for stronger
constructions~\cite{elkouss2009,borisov2023}. One development realization
leaves training uncertainty unmeasured; the transfer pairs couple sample
size with asymmetry and use one QBER. The five evaluation libraries measure
matrix variation. A competitive-code comparison, repeated development
cohorts, and crossed prior/selector choices would be needed to isolate a
broader coding advantage. The upper prior clip assumes errors are less
likely than correct bits; that boundary is inactive in the audited cohorts.
OSD is evaluated in the circuit study, while transfer uses BP alone.

\paragraph{Operational and security interpretation.}
The studies measure separate uses of the same conditional channel; they do
not implement a joint diagnostic-and-decoding procedure. Any combined
system would need to pay for diagnostic acquisition and test how long its
estimates remain useful under drift. A zero-noise residual cannot replace
operating error probabilities in a decoder.
Likewise, $e_0$ and $Y$ are engineering outcomes, not secure-key rates.
Security requires source and detector assumptions, the public transcript,
phase-error estimation, Eve's side information, finite-key analysis and
privacy amplification
\cite{gllp2004,scarani2009,xu2020,fung2009,tomamichel2012,rennerkoenig2005,shor2000}.
These requirements remain separate from improved classical recovery or
model-based disturbance estimation.

\section{Conclusion}
Aggregate QBER can conceal information that is already present in disclosed
BB84 data. Gate-path structure determines whether retaining those labels
identifies the residual disturbance; a rank and row-space criterion makes
that question explicit. Budget-matched experiments then show when operating
observations or additional scaling give the better estimate. The recovery
study carries the same principle into finite samples: useful conditional
structure must repay its estimation and disclosure costs, and rate-policy
gains must be distinguished from fixed-code gains.

The resulting message is a sequence for using channel information:
retain the available observations, establish what they identify, measure
their precision under a common budget, and evaluate their utility at the
final task. The model, complete controls and negative results make each
step of that sequence testable.
